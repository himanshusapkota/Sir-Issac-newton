Isaac Newton

Mathematician, physicist, astronomer and natural philosopher (1643–1727)

Early Life

Isaac Newton was born in 1643 in Woolsthorpe, Lincolnshire, England. He was born prematurely, and his father had already died before he was born. Newton's childhood was not particularly easy. His mother later remarried, and for some time he was left in the care of his grandmother.

As a young student, Newton attended The King's School in Grantham. He eventually went to Trinity College, Cambridge, in 1661. University life introduced him to mathematics, philosophy and astronomy, although he was not always interested in simply following what his teachers taught.

He was especially interested in ideas from people such as Galileo, Copernicus and Descartes. Newton liked to investigate things for himself, and this habit became an important part of his later scientific work.

The Plague Years

In 1665, the plague spread through England and Cambridge University was temporarily closed. Newton returned to his family home in Woolsthorpe. It sounds like a quiet period, but it was actually one of the most important periods of his life.

While working mostly by himself, Newton developed many of the ideas that would later become extremely important in mathematics and physics. He worked on what he called the "method of fluxions", which became part of what we now know as calculus.

He also experimented with light and colors and thought about gravity and the motion of the Moon. A lot of these ideas were still unfinished, but they eventually became the foundation for some of his greatest work.

"If I have seen further than others, it is by standing on the shoulders of giants."

The Principia Mathematica

Newton's work on motion and gravity became much more serious in the 1680s. In 1684, astronomer Edmond Halley encouraged him to investigate the problem of planetary motion. Newton eventually produced a much larger work than Halley had originally expected.

In 1687, Newton published Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica, usually called the Principia. It became one of the most important scientific books ever written.

In the book, Newton described his three laws of motion and his law of universal gravitation. His theory explained that the force which makes objects fall toward Earth is related to the force that keeps the Moon in orbit.

Newton's Three Laws of Motion

  • First Law: An object will remain at rest or keep moving at a constant speed unless a force acts on it.
  • Second Law: The acceleration of an object depends on its mass and the force acting on it. This is commonly written as F = ma.
  • Third Law: For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.

Optics and the Reflecting Telescope

Newton did not only study gravity. He was also fascinated by light. One of his famous experiments involved passing sunlight through a glass prism. The white light separated into different colors, showing that white light is actually made up of a mixture of colors.

He later used another prism to show that these colors could be combined again to produce white light. This helped change the way scientists understood color.

Newton also worked on telescopes. In 1668, he built a reflecting telescope that used a mirror instead of a large lens. This helped reduce a problem called chromatic aberration, which affected many telescopes at the time.

His ideas and experiments about light were eventually published in his book Opticks in 1704.

Later Life

Newton's life was not limited to experiments and books. He became the Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at Cambridge in 1669, a very respected position that is still associated with famous scientists today.

Later, Newton moved into government work. He became Warden of the Royal Mint and eventually its Master. He took the problem of counterfeit money seriously and was involved in efforts to protect England's currency.

In 1703, he became President of the Royal Society. Two years later, Queen Anne knighted him, making him Sir Isaac Newton.

Newton remained a private person for much of his life. He never married. He died in 1727 in Kensington, London, and was buried in Westminster Abbey.

The Apple Story The famous story says an apple falling from a tree gave Newton the idea for gravity. The story has probably been exaggerated over time, although Newton did talk about watching apples fall.
Calculus Dispute Newton and German mathematician Gottfried Leibniz both developed versions of calculus. Their supporters later became involved in a long and bitter argument about who had developed it first.
Alchemy Newton spent a surprising amount of time studying alchemy. He also studied biblical chronology and other subjects that would not normally be considered science today.
The South Sea Bubble Newton invested money in the South Sea Company and lost a significant amount when the bubble collapsed in 1720.

His Legacy

Newton's influence on science is difficult to overstate. His laws of motion and theory of universal gravitation became the foundation of classical physics for more than two centuries.

Later scientists, including Albert Einstein, showed that Newton's ideas were not the complete description of nature, especially when dealing with extremely high speeds, massive objects or very strong gravity. Even so, Newton's work is still extremely useful today.

His work in mathematics was just as important. Calculus became an essential tool for physics, engineering, mathematics and many other fields.

The poet Alexander Pope summed up Newton's reputation with these famous lines:

"Nature and Nature's laws lay hid in night; God said, 'Let Newton be!' and all was light."
